Types of phishing

The delivery method changes, the goal stays the same: make you act without checking.

Email phishing

Mass emails pretending to be banks, shops or delivery services.

Spear phishing

Targeted at one person, using details like your name, job or manager.

Smishing

Phishing by SMS, such as a fake parcel fee or bank alert with a link.

Vishing

Phone calls from someone claiming to be your bank or IT support.

Fake websites

Copies of real login pages built to capture what you type.

How to recognise a phishing email

Odd sender address

Look at the full address, not only the display name.

Urgent or threatening tone

“Act now”, “last warning”, “account suspended”.

Generic greeting

“Dear Customer” instead of your name.

Spelling and design errors

Poor grammar, blurry logos, strange layout.

Unexpected links or attachments

Especially .zip, .exe, or “invoice” files you did not request.

Requests for secrets

Passwords, OTPs, PINs and card numbers should never be sent by email.

How to recognise a fake website

A padlock or HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted. It does not mean the site is honest. Check the domain itself.

https://secure-login.paypal.account-verify.comFake. The real domain is the part before the first single slash: account-verify.com.
https://www.paypal.com/signinReal. The domain is paypal.com.
https://arnazon.comFake. “rn” looks like “m”.
https://www.amazon.comReal.

More checks

  • Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark.
  • Be careful with shortened links.
  • Be suspicious of pop-ups asking you to log in.

Site red flags

  • Misspelled or extra words in the domain
  • Unusual endings such as .top or .xyz for a big brand
  • Page asks for far more data than needed

Social engineering tactics

Attackers exploit emotions, not only technology. If a message makes you feel rushed, scared or excited, pause.

Urgency

“Respond within 1 hour or lose access.”

Fear

“Suspicious login detected on your account.”

Authority

A message that appears to come from your boss, bank or the police.

Greed

“You won a prize. Pay a small fee to claim it.”

Curiosity and helpfulness

“Is this you in the photo?” or “Can you do me a quick favour?”

Common real-world scam patterns

These are typical scenarios, rewritten as examples. Names and links are fictional.

The parcel fee SMS

“Your parcel is held. Pay a 150 fee here: short-link.example.” Goal: steal card details. Check the courier’s official app instead.

The bank alert

“Your card is blocked. Call or log in to restore.” Goal: capture banking login or OTP. Banks never ask for your OTP.

The prize or lottery

“Congratulations, you won!” You never entered. Goal: collect fees and personal data.

The boss request

“I’m in a meeting, buy gift cards and send me the codes.” Goal: quick money. Confirm by calling the person.

Protect yourself

Do

  • Pause and verify through a second channel
  • Use a password manager and unique passwords
  • Turn on two-factor authentication
  • Keep your phone and apps updated
  • Report suspicious messages to IT or your provider

Don’t

  • Click links or open attachments you did not expect
  • Share OTPs, PINs or passwords with anyone
  • Trust a message only because it looks official
  • Log in through a link sent to you
  • Reuse the same password everywhere

If you already clicked

  1. Disconnect from the network if you downloaded a file.
  2. Change the password on the real site, and anywhere you reused it.
  3. Contact your bank if you entered card or banking details.
  4. Run an antivirus scan.
  5. Report it to your IT team or the service being imitated.
